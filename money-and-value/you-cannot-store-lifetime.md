# You Cannot Store "Lifetime"

“Money is not only the medium in which we store wealth. It is the unit in which we exchange our lifetime with one another.”

This sounds persuasive because time is genuinely scarce. Every person has a finite life, every hour spent is unrecoverable, and much of what we call work consists of exchanging time and effort for money. From there, it is tempting to conclude that money somehow contains the value of the time used to acquire it, and that inflation therefore represents a debasement of one’s accumulated lifetime.

The problem is that this assumes there is some fair or objective value of a person’s lifetime to preserve. There is not. Value is subjective, and the same hour of labor may be worth nothing to one person, $20 to another, and $20,000 to someone who urgently needs exactly what that person can provide. The hour is identical in each case. What changes is the counterparty, the circumstances, the alternatives available, and what is known or believed about the person making the offer.

This is where Bitcoin-derivative thinking can become limiting. Bitcoin teaches us to think carefully about monetary supply, scarcity, and debasement, but those concepts do not imply that value itself is an objective quantity that can be preserved inside a sufficiently hard asset. Bitcoin can make the quantity of bitcoin predictable. It cannot make the future value of anything predictable.

There is no container for value. There are only claims, counterparties, and changing judgments about what those claims are worth.

## Value is a relationship, not a substance

When you work for $100, you have not converted several hours of your lifetime into $100 according to some universal exchange rate. You have found a counterparty who prefers what you can provide to the $100 they currently possess, while you prefer the $100 to whatever else you could have done with that time.

Another person may have offered you $50. Someone else may have offered $1,000. Most people would have offered nothing because they had no use for your service at all.

Economic value therefore does not originate in the amount of time expended. Time is one scarce input among many. Knowledge, circumstance, trust, scarcity, skill, ownership, relationships, timing, authority, and luck may matter just as much or more.

A useful way to understand the broader structure behind these things is reputation.

By reputation, I do not mean only whether somebody is considered honest, competent, or likable. I mean the recognized standing produced by evidence about a person: what they have done, what they know, what they can do, what they own, who trusts them, who owes them, what institutions recognize their authority, what relationships they possess, and what claims they can credibly present to others.

The distinction between evidence and reputation is useful. A deed, signature, degree, transaction history, or cryptographic key may provide evidence. Reputation is the standing that results when relevant counterparties recognize what that evidence implies.

Under this broader definition, much of economic life is reputational.

A university degree contributes to reputation because an institution attests to some educational history. A credit score summarizes evidence about prior financial behavior. A property title supports a recognized claim over an asset. A brand carries expectations about the things associated with it. Citizenship, contracts, professional credentials, employment history, and ownership records all work in related ways.

Even inheritance fits this model. An heir may have contributed nothing to the production of the inherited wealth, but a recognized relationship connects the previous owner, the heir, the property, and the people or institutions willing to honor the resulting claim. The relevant reputation is not one of productive merit, but of lineage and recognized succession.

The important point is not that all reputation is deserved or earned. It is that economic claims function only insofar as some relevant set of people or systems recognizes the evidence supporting them.

## Money as reputation compression

Money is particularly important because it allows a portion of this complicated reputational structure to become standardized, fungible, and transferable.

Imagine trying to conduct an economy in which every exchange required reconstructing the history that justified each person’s claims. Alice provided a service to Bob, Bob produced something for Carol, Carol transferred property to Dave, Dave fulfilled an obligation to Eve, and so on. If every new counterparty had to verify this entire history, large-scale trade would quickly become impractical.

Money compresses much of that history into a simpler claim.

Instead of explaining why Alice should be able to obtain goods worth $100, Alice can simply possess $100. Instead of reconstructing the productive, commercial, familial, legal, or accidental history that produced a bitcoin balance, a counterparty only needs to verify that the transfer is valid according to Bitcoin’s rules.

In this sense, money makes a subset of reputation transferable. It abstracts away much of the history behind a claim and gives strangers a relatively cheap way to recognize it.

This is one of money’s most important properties. It allows economic standing acquired in one context to travel into another context without requiring every future counterparty to understand how that standing was acquired.

But money does not contain the value represented by that history. It carries a claim that future counterparties may recognize on terms that cannot be known in advance.

## We do not exchange our lifetimes

Seen this way, the claim that money is the unit in which we exchange our lifetimes becomes misleading.

We do not exchange lifetimes. We exchange claims.

Time can contribute to the creation of those claims, but it does not determine their value. Two people may each devote ten thousand hours to their careers and emerge with radically different economic positions. One develops a scarce and useful skill while another develops a skill that becomes obsolete. One builds a successful business while another joins a failing one. One accumulates relationships that create future opportunities. One inherits valuable property. One buys an asset shortly before demand for it increases. One happens to live in an environment where his abilities are highly valued, while another with identical abilities lives somewhere they are not.

Nothing about equal quantities of time requires equal economic outcomes because value does not originate from time.

What actually changes over a person’s life is the set of terms other people are willing to offer them. Those terms are influenced by the accumulation of skills, knowledge, ownership, relationships, credibility, obligations, rights, and other forms of recognized standing.

Your economic position improves when counterparties become willing to make better exchanges with you. That is what reputation means in economic terms.

## The problem with “store of value”

This also makes the phrase “store of value” less straightforward than it initially appears.

What exactly is being stored?

Suppose I earn $100 today and do not expect to spend it for twenty years. I can hold the dollars, buy bitcoin, buy equities, purchase land, buy gold, acquire inventory, invest in a business, pay for education, or spend the money building relationships and capabilities that I believe will benefit me later.

Each choice exposes me to a different future.

If I hold dollars, I am speculating that dollars will remain sufficiently useful relative to the things I eventually want. If I hold bitcoin, I am speculating that future counterparties will value bitcoin highly relative to those same things. If I buy stock, I am speculating on the future productive capacity and reputation of the company. If I buy property, I am speculating on the future usefulness and desirability of that property. If I invest in my own education or business, I am speculating that my future economic position will improve more than it would have if I had held another asset.

None of these actions preserves some independent quantity called “the value of the $100.” They exchange one present claim for another claim whose future characteristics I prefer.

This is why all saving is fundamentally speculative. I do not mean speculative in the pejorative sense of gambling. I mean that every act of saving necessarily involves a judgment about a future that cannot be known in advance.

There is no asset into which economic value can be placed, frozen in time, and later withdrawn unchanged. Subjective value makes such a thing impossible in principle.

## Inflation does not reach backward through time

This also changes how we should think about inflation.

If you worked yesterday and received $100, the economic event was completed yesterday. Your labor was exchanged for a monetary claim under terms that both parties accepted at that time. The value of that labor is not sitting inside the $100 waiting to be preserved.

If the purchasing power of the $100 later falls, the future terms available to the monetary claim have changed. That may be undesirable. It may result from deliberate monetary policy. It may redistribute purchasing power among participants. It may make the money a worse asset to hold.

But it has not reached backward through time and altered the exchange you already made.

This distinction matters because describing inflation as the destruction of stored lifetime implies that labor has some persistent objective value that money is responsible for carrying forward unchanged. Once we reject that premise, the issue becomes more precise: inflation changes the future usefulness of a monetary claim. It does not debase an objective quantity of past human time.

That is still a perfectly legitimate reason to prefer money with more predictable supply. It simply does not require a false theory of value.

## Saving is a choice among future reputations

A more precise description of saving is that we exchange present claims for claims on future reputation.

A person who buys bitcoin exchanges a presently recognized claim for another claim whose future monetary reputation he expects to be stronger. A person who buys stock exchanges money for a claim on the future productive capacity and reputation of a company. A person who buys land does the same with property. A lender exchanges present resources for another person’s promise because he expects that promise to remain credible enough to be honored later.

The same logic applies when somebody invests in himself. Education, skills, relationships, health, and experience can all increase the future terms available from counterparties.

We are therefore not preserving an objective quantity of value through time. We are continually choosing among different claims, different forms of reputation, and different possible futures.

## What Bitcoin actually solves

None of this diminishes the importance of Bitcoin. It simply narrows the claim to something Bitcoin can actually guarantee.

Bitcoin provides a monetary asset whose issuance is highly predictable and whose supply cannot be arbitrarily expanded by an administrator. Nobody can decide tomorrow that an additional ten million bitcoin should be created and distributed to a preferred constituency. This removes an important source of uncertainty and political discretion from the monetary system.

That is a remarkable property.

It also gives Bitcoin a useful quality as a measure within its own economy. Changes in bitcoin-denominated prices cannot be attributed to an administrator unexpectedly changing the monetary base according to political preference. One major variable has been constrained by the protocol rather than delegated to discretion.

What Bitcoin does not provide is a fixed relationship between bitcoin and everything else.

Bitcoin cannot guarantee how much food one bitcoin will buy in twenty years. It cannot guarantee how many hours of another person’s labor someone will exchange for it. It cannot guarantee that people will continue to prefer bitcoin over equities, land, gold, dollars, another blockchain, or an asset that has not yet been invented.

Bitcoin fixes its supply. It does not fix human preference.

Since value ultimately depends on preference, fixed supply cannot guarantee fixed value.

## Absolute scarcity is not absolute scarcity of economic choice

This distinction is especially important when discussing Bitcoin’s scarcity.

Bitcoin can be absolutely scarce according to its own rules. There will not suddenly be 42 million bitcoin. However, the economic universe surrounding Bitcoin is not scarce in the same sense.

People can create new currencies, new blockchains, new companies, new forms of equity, new credit instruments, new technologies, and entirely new categories of property. Governments can issue additional fiat currencies. Businesses can create new claims. Communities can develop new accounting systems. Technologies can make previously valuable assets less useful.

None of this inflates the supply of bitcoin.

But it does expand the universe of alternatives competing for human demand.

This gives us an important distinction:

**Absolute scarcity is not absolute scarcity of economic choice.**

Bitcoin can constrain its own quantity, but it cannot constrain the number of alternatives that people may choose to value. It cannot guarantee that humanity will allocate some fixed proportion of its wealth, productive capacity, or attention to bitcoin merely because bitcoin itself is scarce.

Bitcoin therefore has reputation too.

Its users and potential users form judgments about its monetary policy, security, liquidity, censorship resistance, history, usefulness, survivability, and future adoption. Those judgments affect the terms on which people are willing to exchange other things for bitcoin.

Scarcity matters enormously because it constrains what can happen to supply once demand exists. But scarcity alone does not create demand. The world contains many things that are rare, unique, or impossible to reproduce and nevertheless economically worthless.

Bitcoin’s value comes from scarcity interacting with reputation, not scarcity replacing reputation.

## The Bitcoin-derivative trap

The mistake is to take properties that are extremely useful within Bitcoin and promote them into universal economic laws.

Bitcoin has a fixed supply, therefore fixed supply preserves value. Bitcoin is scarce, therefore scarcity preserves labor. Bitcoin cannot be monetarily debased, therefore bitcoin cannot debase your lifetime. Bitcoin has performed well as savings, therefore sufficiently hard money can remove speculation from saving.

None of these conclusions follows from Bitcoin’s actual properties.

Bitcoin solves the problem of discretionary monetary issuance extraordinarily well. It does not solve subjective value, uncertainty, changing preferences, technological change, or the existence of competing economic claims.

It does not need to.

Bitcoin is already important because it allows people to control a scarce digital asset under rules that are unusually resistant to arbitrary revision. It provides strong evidence of ownership and authorization and allows claims to move between strangers without requiring those strangers to reconstruct the reputational history that produced each balance.

That is a substantial achievement without requiring us to claim that Bitcoin stores human time.

## What we actually accumulate

If we do not accumulate stored lifetime, what do we accumulate?

Over time, we build an economic position composed of many different things: skills, knowledge, relationships, ownership, credibility, capabilities, contractual rights, access, experience, optionality, money, and evidence of past behavior. Some of these things are transferable and some are not. Some can be inherited. Some exist only because a specific community recognizes them. Some disappear quickly, while others can survive for generations.

Together, they form the reputational state from which other people decide whether they want to interact with us and on what terms.

Money is one particularly convenient component of that state because it is relatively easy to transfer between strangers. Bitcoin is an especially interesting form of money because its monetary rules are unusually difficult for any particular party to change.

Neither property means that money contains an objective quantity of human value.

The intuition that inflation debases our lifetime is understandable because watching purchasing power decline can feel as though past effort is being erased. But the past effort was exchanged when the work occurred. What remains afterward is a claim, and from that point forward we must decide what form of claim we wish to hold next.

Sometimes dollars would have been the better choice. Sometimes bitcoin. Sometimes equities, gold, oil, land, a business, education, or immediate consumption would have produced the better outcome. Examples of all of these can be found continuously because no asset has a fixed relationship to every future human preference.

This is not a failure of money. It is the unavoidable consequence of subjective value.

Bitcoin does not abolish that uncertainty, and no monetary system can. What Bitcoin gives us is something narrower and more defensible: a monetary claim whose quantity cannot be arbitrarily changed by somebody else.

Your lifetime is not stored in your wallet. Your wealth is not a container filled with accumulated hours. Economic life is a changing network of reputation and claims, continuously evaluated by counterparties whose own circumstances, preferences, and opportunities are changing at the same time.

Money helps us navigate that network, and Bitcoin gives us an unusually robust form of money with which to do so.

But no money can preserve the value of your lifetime, because your lifetime never had an objective price to preserve.
